\begin{abstract}
Using inverse trigonometry to paramaterize simple harmonic motion.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

\begin{example} \label{Qpvce30}
Suppose that between consecutive noons on July 21 and July 22 the depth of the water at the Edmonds pier oscillates in simple harmonic motion between $5$ feet and $21$ feet with a period of $12$ hours. Suppose also that at 3am on July 21 the water is $11$ feet deep and falling.

Use inverse trigonometry to help find a function
\[
  h = f(t) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq 24
\]
that expresses the depth of the water in terms of the number of hours since noon on July 21.

\begin{explanation}
We'll use the cosine function and first express the depth in terms of the polar angle:
\[
  h = g(\theta) = 13 + 8\cos\theta.
\]

To express the polar angle $\theta = a(t)$ as a function of time, we first find a possible value $a(15)$ for the polar angle $a(15) = \theta_0$ at 3am on July 22. Since the water is 11 feet deep at 3am,
\[
  h = 13 + 8\cos\theta_0 = 11
\]
and
\[
  \cos\theta_0 = -1/4.
\]
This equation has infinitely many solutions, but we'll choose the one between $0$ and $\pi$, namely
\[
 \theta_ 0 = \arccos(-1/4).
\]

Now since the tide is going out at 3am, the function
\[
  h = g(\theta) = 13 + 8\cos\theta
\]
is decreasing when $\theta=\theta_0 =  \arccos(-1/4)$. And because $\cos\theta$ is a decreasing function for $\theta\in [0,\pi]$,  $\theta$ is increasing when $\theta=\theta_0$. That means the rotation rate of the polar angle is positive. Since the tides osciallate with a period of $12$ hours, the rotation rate of the uniform circluar motion that drives the oscillation is
\[
  \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{2\pi \text{ rad}}{12\text{ hrs}} = \frac{\pi}{6} \text{ rad/hr}.
\]
So the polar angle function is
\begin{align*}
  \theta &= a(t) \\
           &= a(15) + \frac{\pi}{6}\left( t-15 \right) \\
           &= \arccos(-1/4) + \frac{\pi}{6}\left( t-15 \right) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq 24. 
\end{align*}
And therefore the function
\begin{align*}
   h &= g(a(t)) \\
      &= g\left( \arccos(-1/4) + \frac{\pi}{6}\left( t-15 \right) \right)  \\
      &= 13 + 8\cos \left(  \arccos(-1/4) + \frac{\pi}{6}\left( t-15 \right) \right) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq 24,
\end{align*}
expresses the depth of the water (in feet) in terms of the number of hours since noon on July 21.
\end{explanation}
\end{example}

\begin{question} \label{Qoer333fr}
Suppose that between consecutive noons on July 21 and July 22 the depth of the water at the Edmonds pier oscillates in simple harmonic motion between $5$ feet and $21$ feet with a period of $12$ hours. Suppose also that at 3am on July 21 the water is $11$ feet deep and falling.

Use inverse trigonometry to help find a function
\[
  h = f(t) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq 24
\]
that expresses the depth of the water in terms of the number of hours since noon on July 21.

Solution:

Remember the key idea. That a constant speed motion around a circle drives simple harmonic motion along a diameter of that circle. So to model the depth of the water we start by choosing the circle of motion and a diameter of that circle.

I like to imagine the water oscillating vertically, so I'll label the upward-pointing axis as the positive $h$-axis and keep the $x$-axis horizontal. Both are measured in feet.

%In the standard $xy$-coordinate system with the positive $y$-axis pointing upward, we'll relabel the $y$-axis as the $h$-axis and imagine the water to oscillate vertically.

We now describe our circle of motion.


Its center is at a point with $h$-coordinate
\[
   h  =  \frac{1}{2}\left( 21 \text{ ft} + 5 \text{ ft} \right) = 13 \text{ ft}
\]
equal to the average depth of the water over a 12-hour period.

It has radius
\[
   \frac{1}{2} \left( 21 \text{ ft} - 5 \text{ ft} \right) = 8\text{ ft} ,
\]
equal to the maximum deviation from the average depth.

The $x$-coordinate of this circle is immaterial and we'll suppose the circle is centered at the point $C$ with coordinates $(0,13)$ (all coordinates in ft) as shown below.

\end{onlineOnly}

Now let $A$ be one of the two points on the circle of motion with $h$ coordinate $h=11$. We'll arbitrarily choose the point in the first quadrant and focus our attention on the vector $\overrightarrow{CA}$. Since $C$ has coordinates $(0,13)$, $\overrightarrow{CA}$ has $h$-component
\[
    h = 11 - 13 = -2 .
\]
Let $x$ be the $x$-component of $\overrightarrow{CA}$. Then $\overrightarrow{CA} = \langle x, -2 \rangle$. And since $|\overrightarrow{CA}| = 8$,
\[
x^2 + (-2)^2 = 8^2
\]
and 
\[
  x = \pm \sqrt{60}.
\]
But because $A$ is in the first quadrant 
\[
    x = \sqrt{60}
\]
and
\[
\overrightarrow{CA} = \langle \sqrt{60},-2\rangle.
\]


Up to this point, the solution has been identical to what we did before. But here's where the change comes. We'll let $P$ be the point on the circle that drives the oscillation of the tides. Now we'll express the components of the vector $\overrightarrow{CP}$ in terms of the  \emph{polar} angle $\theta$ of the vector $\overrightarrow{CP}$, measured in the usual way from $\overrightarrow{CW}$ (pointing in the direction of the positive $x$-axis) to $\overrightarrow{CP}$. 

How is this strategy different from our prior solution?
\begin{freeResponse}
\end{freeResponse}

This way makes it easier to get the components of $\overrightarrow{CP}$. Because $\theta$ is the polar angle of $\overrightarrow{CP}$, we know
\begin{align*}
 \overrightarrow{CP} &=  \left| \overrightarrow{CP} \right| \langle \cos\theta , \sin\theta  \rangle    \\
                               &= \langle 8\cos\theta, 8 \sin\theta   \rangle .
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
 \overrightarrow{OP}  &= \overrightarrow{OC} + \overrightarrow{CP} \\
                                &=\langle 0, 13\rangle + \langle 8\cos\theta, 8 \sin\theta   \rangle \\
                                & = \langle 8\cos\theta, 13 + 8 \sin\theta   \rangle . 
\end{align*}

Now the last step is to express $\theta$ as a function of time.

For this, we need to find a polar angle for the vector $\overrightarrow{OA}$. We'll call this angle $\alpha$. From the picture we can take $-\pi/2 < \alpha < 0$. Then since
\[
    \sin \alpha = -\frac{2}{8} ,
\] 
\[
 \alpha = \arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) .
\]

Now since the tide is falling at 3:00am, the point $P$ that drives the oscillation rotates clockwise about the center of its circle. And since the period of oscillation is $12$ hours, $P$ rotates about the center of its circle at the signed rate of
\[
     - \frac{2\pi \text{ rad}}{12\text{ hrs}} = - \frac{\pi}{6} \frac{\text{rad}}{\text{hr}} .
\]
And since 
\[
  \theta=\arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)
\]
at time $t=3$ hours past midnight,
\[
  \theta = a(t) = \arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) -  \frac{\pi}{6} \left(t-3\right) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq 24.
\]

So
\begin{align*}
    \overrightarrow{OP} & = \langle  8\cos\theta, 13 + 8 \sin\theta \rangle \\
                                  & = \langle  8\cos \left(  \arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) -  \frac{\pi}{6} \left(t-3\right) \right), 13 + 8 \sin \left( \arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) -  \frac{\pi}{6} \left(t-3\right) \right) \rangle .
\end{align*}

But remember, it's the $h$-component (ie. the second component) of this vector that expresses the depth of the water as a function of time. So the function 
\[
   h = f(t) =  13 + 8 \sin \left( \arcsin \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) -  \frac{\pi}{6} \left(t-3\right) \right) \, , \, 0\leq t \leq  24,
\]
expresses the depth of the water (in feet) in terms of the number of hours past midnight, July 21.

You should check this by 
\begin{enumerate}

\item checking that $f(3) = 11$ and

\item by inputting this function (named $f_1(t)$) in Line 4 of the worksheet above.

\end{enumerate}
\end{question}

\begin{exercise} \label{Ex39v39}
Suppose between consecutive midnights the temperature in Seattle oscillates in simple harmonic motion about its mean between a high of $83^\circ$F and a low of $51^\circ$F with a period of 24 hours. Suppose also the temperature is $79^\circ$F at 2pm and rising.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Follow the method of Example 1 to find a function 
\[
  T = f(h) \, , \, 0\leq h \leq 24,
\]
that expresses the temperature (in Fahrenheit degrees) in terms of the number of hours past midnight.

\item Use your function to find the exact time(s) when the temperature is $60^\circ$F. Then use a calculator to approximate these times to the nearest minute.

\item Determine the exact time when the temperature is a maximum. Then use a calculator to approximate this time.

\item Find the exact fraction of the $24$-hour period during which the temperature is at least $60^\circ$F. Then use a calculator to approximate this fraction to the nearest hundredth.
\end{enumerate}

\end{exercise}
